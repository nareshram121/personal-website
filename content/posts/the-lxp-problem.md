I spent a good stretch of my career as Chief Platform Officer at Techademy, leading product for an end-to-end Learning Experience Platform built for IT companies in India. Content, proctored assessments, mentoring, labs, community, gamification, even job postings. We tried to put the whole journey in one place.

That experience left me with strong opinions about corporate learning platforms. Some of them are uncomfortable for people who sell these platforms, and some are uncomfortable for people who buy them. I have been on the building side, so I will try to be fair. But here is my honest view: most corporate learning platforms fail, and they fail for reasons that have very little to do with technology.

## A content library is not learning

The most common mistake is to treat a learning platform as a library. Buy or build thousands of courses, organise them neatly, make them searchable, and assume learning will follow.

It does not. A library is a fine thing to have. I love libraries; I have built one. But nobody becomes a better engineer because they have access to a catalogue. Access was never the bottleneck. Most people in IT already have more free tutorials, videos and documentation than they could consume in a lifetime.

When a platform leads with "we have ten thousand courses", it is answering a question nobody asked. The real questions are different. What should this person learn next? Why should they care? How will they know they have learnt it? Who will help them when they are stuck? A catalogue answers none of these.

## Completion metrics lie

If you want to see how a learning platform is really doing, do not look at its dashboard. Most dashboards are built around completion: courses started, courses finished, hours logged. These numbers are easy to collect and easy to put in a quarterly review. They are also mostly meaningless.

People finish courses for all sorts of reasons. Sometimes they learnt something. Often they were told to, or a deadline loomed, or they let the video play in a background tab while they did their actual work. Completion measures compliance, not capability.

I have come to believe that a learning metric is only worth tracking if it would change if someone genuinely got better at their job. Can they now pass a hands-on assessment they could not pass before? Can they complete a task in a lab environment? Has their manager noticed a difference? These are harder to measure. That is precisely why they matter.

## Learning needs a reason

Adults do not learn in a vacuum. Children often do not either, and I have spent enough time in schools to know that "because it is on the syllabus" is a weak motivator for anyone.

At work, the reason is usually one of three things: a job, a project, or a person.

- **A job.** Someone wants to move into a new role, and they can see what skills it requires. Linking learning to real openings, internal or external, gives it an obvious point.
- **A project.** A team is about to adopt a new stack, and people need to be productive in it by a certain point. Learning tied to an immediate need sticks, because it is used straight away.
- **A mentor.** Someone more experienced is invested in your growth, checks in on you, and notices when you drift. Very few people sustain learning entirely on their own.

This is why we put job postings and mentoring inside the platform, not as nice extras but as part of the core loop. A learning platform without a reason built in is a gym membership nobody uses after January.

## Practice and assessment are not optional

In technical skills especially, watching is not learning. You can watch someone write code for hours and still freeze when you face a blank editor. Skill comes from doing, getting it wrong, and fixing it.

That is why I put so much weight on labs: real environments where learners can build, break and debug things, rather than multiple-choice quizzes that test recall. And it is why assessment matters, including proctored assessment where the stakes justify it. Not because we want to catch people out, but because an honest assessment tells everyone, the learner included, where they actually stand. A credential means something only if it is hard to fake.

Many platforms shy away from this because it is expensive and difficult to build. Labs need infrastructure. Good assessments need careful design. It is much easier to ship another video course. But skipping the hard part is how you end up with a platform full of "completed" learners who cannot do the work.

## Managers matter more than features

This is the opinion that product people least like to hear, including the product person I used to be.

You can build the most elegant platform in the world, and it will still fail in a team where the manager does not care. If a manager never asks what someone is learning, never makes time for it, and never connects it to real work, the message is clear: this is not important here. No amount of clever design overrides that.

Conversely, I have seen fairly ordinary tools work well in teams where the manager treats learning as part of the job. They set aside time. They ask about it in one-on-ones. They hand people stretch work that uses the new skill. The platform becomes an enabler rather than the whole strategy.

If I were advising a company on where to invest, I would say: spend less time comparing feature lists and more time equipping managers to lead learning. That is where the leverage is.

## Gamification helps only when it rewards the right thing

We built gamification into our platform, and I do not regret it. Points, badges and leaderboards can genuinely help. They give people a sense of progress, a bit of friendly competition, and a reason to come back.

But gamification is an amplifier. It amplifies whatever you choose to reward. Reward time spent, and people will leave videos running. Reward courses completed, and people will rush through the easiest ones. Reward logins, and you will get logins.

The trick is to reward the behaviours that actually indicate learning: passing a difficult lab, helping someone else in the community, improving on a previous assessment score, finishing a project. When the game points at real growth, it works. When it points at activity, it actively makes things worse, because now you have a metric that looks good and a culture optimising for it.

## What it actually takes

None of this is a secret. Most people who have thought seriously about learning would agree with most of it. The problem is that platforms are bought and sold on things that are easy to demonstrate: catalogue size, slick interfaces, attractive dashboards. The things that actually drive learning, such as reasons, practice, honest assessment, mentors and managers, are harder to show in a sales meeting.

> A learning platform should be judged by what people can do afterwards, not by what they clicked on.

If you are building one, my advice is to design around the learner's reason for being there and to build the hard parts first. If you are buying one, ask uncomfortable questions about outcomes, and be suspicious of any answer that is mostly about completion rates.

And whichever side you are on, remember that learning is a human activity. The platform can make it easier. It cannot make it happen.
